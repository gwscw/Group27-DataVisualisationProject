Glossary & Data Dictionary (BITRE / AIHW Standards)

This dashboard presents hospitalised injuries in Australia where the cause was a road crash, sourced from the Bureau of Infrastructure and Transport Research Economics (BITRE) and AIHW National Hospital Morbidity Database (2011-2021).

Hospitalised injuries (Separations & Admission Criteria)

A hospitalised injury is defined as an episode of care for an admitted patient ending between 1 Jan 2011 and 31 Dec 2021, coded under ICD-10-AM. To minimise double counting where a person experiences multiple care episodes, hospital transfers and non-acute statistical admissions are excluded from case counts.

Why ALOS Differs: Transfer Linking & Bed Days Accumulation

Average Length of Stay (ALOS) is total patient bed days divided by hospitalisation cases. Per official BITRE specifications: When an individual receives care ending in transfer to another hospital, only the first separation counts as 1 case, but the bed days of all transferred periods of care are combined. This mechanism naturally elevates ALOS in regional and remote cohorts (e.g. Northern Territory and remote First Nations communities) that require long-distance medical evacuation and multi-hospital care pathways. Between 2011 and 2021, remote First Nations crash patients averaged 6.08 bed days per admission (peaking at 7.04 days in 2017) compared to 4.55 days in major cities, reflecting complex trauma care and aeromedical transit via the Royal Flying Doctor Service (RFDS) to capital city hospitals.

First Nations Road Trauma Disparity by Remoteness

In major cities, First Nations individuals represent approximately 2% of annual road traffic hospitalisations, matching urban demographic proportions. However, in remote areas, First Nations Australians accounted for 45.6% of all crash hospitalisations by 2021 (503 out of 1,103 admissions), despite representing roughly 3.8% of the national population. Contributing factors include higher travel speeds, unsealed road networks, older vehicle profiles, and limited roadside barrier infrastructure.

The 2020 COVID-19 Divergence in Remote Areas

During the 2020 pandemic lockdowns, metropolitan traffic volumes dropped sharply, reducing urban crash admissions. In contrast, remote First Nations hospitalisations reached a 10-year high of 504 admissions. This divergence highlights that essential travel over long distances and unsealed roads continued uninterrupted in remote communities, independent of urban travel reductions.

Data Breaks in Series: Emergency Department Policy Revisions

Victoria revised its admission criteria from 1 July 2012 to exclude episodes delivered solely within Emergency Departments (reducing Victorian admissions by ~5.6%). NSW implemented an equivalent policy change from June 2017. These policy shifts introduce structural breaks in annual time-series comparisons.

Remoteness Area (Usual Residence vs Crash Location)

Remoteness areas follow the Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS) Remoteness Structure. In this dataset, remoteness strictly refers to the usual residence of the patient, not the physical location of the crash. Extended stays for remote residents often reflect subacute care deficits and discharge delays prior to returning home.

Graphical Integrity & Population Limitations

BITRE reports absolute injury counts. Direct comparisons of raw counts between jurisdictions (e.g. NT vs VIC) or demographic cohorts (e.g. First Nations ~3.8% of national population) reflect population size rather than risk. Rate-based analysis and ALOS metrics provide critical equity insights free of population scale distortions.
